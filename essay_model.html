Paragraph 1 — Guernica (WHAT → HOW → MESSAGE)

Guernica is a large-scale oil painting rendered entirely in black, white and grey, depicting a chaotic arrangement of distorted human and animal figures including a screaming woman holding a dead child, a wounded horse, and fragmented bodies. The composition is crowded, with overlapping forms and sharp, angular shapes compressed across a single horizontal plane. The monochrome palette removes warmth and resembles the imagery of newspaper photography, reinforcing associations with death and documentation rather than art. The sharp, broken forms create instability and visual chaos, reflecting the destruction and panic of the bombing — exaggerated expressions, open mouths and wide eyes signal extreme pain. The crowded composition creates claustrophobia, making the viewer feel trapped inside the violence rather than observing it from a safe distance. Through a lens of trauma theory (Judith Herman, Trauma and Recovery, 1992), the painting does not merely represent suffering — it enacts it: the fractured composition mirrors the psychological state of the survivor, unable to order memory into linear narrative. Guernica does not show war; it performs the experience of those who cannot un-see it.

Paragraph 2 — The Third of May, 1808 (WHAT → HOW → MESSAGE)

Goya's The Third of May, 1808 depicts a group of civilians being executed by a line of soldiers at night. The soldiers stand in rigid formation with their backs to the viewer, their rifles aimed at a man in a white shirt who raises his arms; a lantern on the ground provides the only source of light. The strong contrast between light and dark draws immediate attention to the central figure — Goya uses chiaroscuro to create a dramatic focal point that commands emotional response. The white clothing of the condemned man functions symbolically: white conventionally signifies innocence, creating an ironic visual argument that those being killed are the morally pure, while the mechanical soldiers — faceless, uniform, without individuality — represent the brutality of state violence. Michel Foucault argued in Discipline and Punish (1975) that modern power operates not through physical spectacle but through systematic control of bodies — Goya's painting anticipates this argument: the soldiers are disciplined machines, the civilian is a body controlled entirely by the state's decision to eliminate it. The painting communicates that war is not heroic — it is a mechanism through which the powerful exercise absolute power over the powerless.

Paragraph 3 — Synthesis (COMPARE → CONTRAST HOW → ALIGN MESSAGE)

Both Guernica and The Third of May, 1808 present responses to state violence that centre on civilian suffering rather than heroism, although they differ fundamentally in style and structure. Guernica is abstract and chaotic — multiple distorted figures in a fragmented, non-realist composition — while The Third of May is figurative and focused, depicting a single moment of execution with clear roles of victim and aggressor. Picasso uses distortion, sharp angles, and a monochrome palette to generate visual confusion that mirrors the experience of violence. Goya, in contrast, uses controlled lighting and a single focal point to guide the viewer's gaze deliberately toward one man's terror — achieving emotional impact through clarity rather than chaos. Despite these contrasting methods, the messages of both works are closely aligned. Together, they communicate that war is destructive, dehumanising, and primarily harms ordinary people — both works reject any notion of conflict as heroic or glorious. Read through Foucault's framework, both artists reveal the same underlying argument: that political violence is not an aberration but a function of how power operates on the body. While Picasso presents this as overwhelming disorder and Goya as controlled precision, both refuse to let the viewer look away from the human cost.
